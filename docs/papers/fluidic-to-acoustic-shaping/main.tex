\begin{abstract}
Fluidic shaping, introduced by Bercovici and co-workers, forms optical
components from a curable liquid pinned on a bounding frame inside a
nearly density-matched immersion liquid; the interface is a minimum of
interfacial plus gravitational energy at fixed volumes, and it is then cured.
The reachable surfaces are therefore solutions of the capillary--gravity
(Young--Laplace) equation: spherical caps at neutral buoyancy, Bessel-type
aspheres at finite Bond number, and Fourier--Bessel freeforms set by the
frame height. We pose the extension as an inverse problem: add a normal
traction field to the interface and ask which traction holds a prescribed
surface. Stationarity of the pinned, volume-constrained energy gives this
\emph{load demand} in closed form, unique up to one constant per independent
volume constraint. Near the axis it reduces to a short series; for conic faces
the leading term vanishes when $\Delta\rho g/\sigma=4kc^2$, and the remainder
shows that no non-spherical conic is an unforced equilibrium. We then propose
acoustic radiation stress as the means of applying the load. In a cylinder
with ideal velocity sources the generalized force is a Hermitian form in the
source amplitudes; multi-tone drives turn the reachable forces into a convex
cone, so the best attainable tolerance is a semidefinite program with a dual
certificate of impossibility, a rank bound on the number of tones, and convex
pressure and fold-margin constraints. A fold-margin inequality also implies
creeping-flow stability for every symmetric positive mobility. Holding a target
does not imply reaching it from the unforced meniscus; we give a sufficient
monotonicity condition for the start-up path. All statements are theoretical
and conditional on an axisymmetric, bulk-lossless, ideal-source, creeping-flow
reduction; acoustic streaming, heating, three-dimensional disturbances,
transducers and curing remain open. Nothing described here has been built or
measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Frumkin and Bercovici showed that a curable liquid injected into a bounding
frame immersed in a second, immiscible liquid of nearly equal density takes a
shape set by interfacial tension, the injected volume and the frame, and can
then be cured into a lens with nanometric surface roughness
\cite{frumkin2021}. Elgarisi \emph{et al.} extended the method to freeform
surfaces by giving the frame an azimuthally varying height
\cite{elgarisi2021,elgarisi2021preprint}. The surfaces that this
\emph{fluidic shaping} reaches are, by construction, equilibria of the
capillary--gravity energy. They form a rich but restricted family: an optical
designer cannot in general prescribe an arbitrary asphere or freeform and
obtain it by choosing a frame, a volume and a density contrast.

This paper asks what must be added to reach a prescribed surface. The answer is
organized as an inverse problem with three steps:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &\text{target surface }h_\star\ \longrightarrow\ \text{load demand }t_\star
 \ \longrightarrow\ \text{acoustic drive }\bm c\\
 &\qquad\longrightarrow\ \text{formation from the unforced meniscus}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The first arrow is exact and explicit (\cref{sec:demand}). The second asks
whether a fixed acoustic source can supply that load; under stated
approximations it becomes a convex feasibility problem with certificates
(\cref{sec:acoustic,sec:capability}). The third asks whether the held
equilibrium is actually the state reached when the drive is switched on from
the fluidic-shaping meniscus, and whether it is stable
(\cref{sec:formation}). \Cref{sec:validity} states the regime in which these
statements hold and the physics that remains open.

\Cref{tab:status} records the status of each result. We use SI units
throughout. The derivations specialize the canonical theory of this project;
where a result is new to this paper it is marked as derived here. Numerical
values quoted from the project's study S09 are illustrations computed in a
declared model, not tests of the propositions and not experimental evidence.

\section{Fluidic shaping}\label{sec:fluidic}

\subsection{The model of Frumkin and Bercovici}
In the configuration of \cite{frumkin2021}, a ring-shaped bounding surface of
radius $R_0$ and vertical thickness $d$ is placed in a chamber filled with an
immersion liquid of density $\rho_{\rm im}$. A curable lens liquid of density
$\rho_{\rm lens}$ is injected into the ring and pins on its edges, forming a
top and a bottom interface with the immersion liquid. Pinning requires only
that the lens liquid adhere to the inner side of the frame more strongly than
the immersion liquid does. Interfacial tension $\gamma$ (we write $\sigma$
below) is taken constant under isothermal conditions. The capillary length
$\ell_c=\sqrt{\gamma/(\Delta\rho g)}$ diverges at neutral buoyancy, so that
interfacial forces dominate at every scale; this is what removes the
millimetre size limit of droplet-based lenses. A thin cylindrical enclosure
beneath the lens traps immersion liquid, and adding or removing it inflates or
deflates the lens from below.

The equilibrium shapes minimize a free-energy functional containing the two
interfacial areas, the gravitational energy of the lens liquid relative to the
immersion liquid, and two Lagrange multipliers: one for the lens volume and one
for the volume enclosed under the lens \cite[eq.~(1)]{frumkin2021}. The
resulting Euler--Lagrange equations are axisymmetric Young--Laplace equations
for the two interfaces, closed by the pinning heights and the two volumes. At
neutral buoyancy both interfaces are spherical caps; the lens is convex when
the injected volume exceeds the frame volume $\pi R_0^2d$ and concave when it
is smaller. A small density mismatch breaks the up--down symmetry while the
surfaces remain close to spherical. For larger mismatch, the authors expand in
$\varepsilon=(h_c/R_0)^2\ll1$, with $h_c$ a characteristic sag, and obtain at
leading order Bessel equations in $x=(r/R_0)\sqrt{\mathrm{Bo}}$, with Bond
number $\mathrm{Bo}=\Delta\rho gR_0^2/\gamma$. One interface is a $J_0$ profile
and the other an $I_0$ profile; in the notation of \cref{sec:twoface} this
reflects the opposite signs of the density step seen by the two faces. They identify four design degrees of freedom: lens
volume, enclosed volume, Bond number and shape of the bounding surface
\cite{frumkin2021}.

The experiments in \cite{frumkin2021} include convex, concave and meniscus
spherical lenses, cylindrical and saddle (toroidal) lenses from a rectangular
frame, bifocal and doublet lenses made in two steps, Bessel-type aspheres, and
a 200\,mm spherical lens. Curing was thermal (PDMS) or by UV
(optical adhesive), and the authors note that curing stresses and shrinkage can
alter the final shape. They report an AFM roughness of 1.15\,nm RMS over
$20\times20$\,\textmu m. They state that the fluidic shapes are stable to
significant disturbances for Bond numbers of order one.

\subsection{Freeform surfaces from the frame}
Elgarisi \emph{et al.} \cite{elgarisi2021,elgarisi2021preprint} let the height
of a cylindrical frame of radius $R_0$ vary with azimuth, $h(R_0,\theta)=
f(\theta)$, and minimize the same kind of energy for a single interface at fixed
volume. In the arXiv version \cite{elgarisi2021preprint}, from which the
following details are taken, the small-slope limit gives a Helmholtz equation
whose solution is a Fourier--Bessel series: each Fourier mode
$a_n\cos n\theta+b_n\sin n\theta$ of the frame height generates a surface mode
proportional to $J_n\bigl((r/R_0)\sqrt{\mathrm{Bo}}\bigr)$, and the volume
enters only through the axisymmetric baseline. As $\mathrm{Bo}\to0$ the modes
become $r^n\cos n\theta$ and $r^n\sin n\theta$, equivalent to the Zernike
polynomials $Z_n^{\pm n}$, on top of a spherical (paraboloidal) baseline. They
fabricated a 36\,mm quatrefoil element at $\mathrm{Bo}=0.2$ with sub-nanometre
RMS roughness in about 40 minutes, and they discuss two-sided freeforms in
different immersion liquids, larger amplitudes by solving the nonlinear
equation, and adaptive elements with deformable frames
\cite{elgarisi2021preprint}. The published article presents an analytical
solution for the topography given a predefined boundary and reports
sub-nanometre roughness \cite{elgarisi2021}; we have not compared its full text
with the preprint line by line.

The authors state two limitations themselves \cite{elgarisi2021preprint}.
Superposition is allowed only over modes sharing one Bond number, which limits
the surfaces that can be fabricated. Also, because the $n$-th mode decays like
$r^n$ toward the centre, the method cannot create high-resolution features at
the centre of the component. The same property makes the surface insensitive
to fine-scale roughness of the frame.

\subsection{What the boundary can and cannot reach}\label{sec:reach}
The following consequence of the model is derived here. Take a face $z=h(x,y)$
over the frame domain $D$, with upward normal, density step
$\Delta\rho=\rho_{\rm below}-\rho_{\rm above}$ and gravitational acceleration
$g$. Define the \emph{capillary--gravity operator}
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D[h]=-\sigma\,\nabla\cdot\frac{\nabla h}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla h|^2}}
       +\Delta\rho\,g\,h ,
 \label{eq:cg-operator}
\end{equation}
in pascals. Every unforced equilibrium satisfies
$\mathcal D[h]=\lambda$ in $D$ for a constant $\lambda$, whatever the frame,
volume or density contrast. The frame enters only through the boundary trace
$h|_{\partial D}$, the volume through $\lambda$, and the liquids through
$\sigma$ and $\Delta\rho$. Hence a target $h_\star$ with a nonconstant
$\mathcal D[h_\star]$ in the interior of $D$ cannot be produced by any frame,
volume or immersion liquid with the same $\sigma,\Delta\rho$. In the small-slope
limit, $\mathcal D[h]\simeq-\sigma\Delta h+\Delta\rho gh$: the frame is
boundary control of a linear elliptic equation, and the reachable surfaces at
fixed $\Delta\rho$ form an affine space (one particular solution plus the
homogeneous solutions with arbitrary boundary data). \Cref{prop:conic} below
gives a concrete instance: no conic other than a sphere is an unforced
equilibrium of any liquid pair.

\section{The load demand of a prescribed surface}\label{sec:demand}

\subsection{Energy with an applied traction}
Add to the fluidic-shaping problem a normal traction $t$ on the interface,
in pascals, positive along the upward normal. Under a virtual normal
displacement $\eta$ it does work $\int_\Gamma t\eta\dd A$. For a graph,
$\eta\dd A=\delta h\dd x\dd y$, since the normal component of a vertical
displacement $\delta h$ is $\delta h/\sqrt{1+|\nabla h|^2}$ and the area element
is $\sqrt{1+|\nabla h|^2}\dd x\dd y$. With the face pinned on $\partial D$ and
its enclosed volume fixed, consider
\begin{equation}
 \mathscr L[h]=\int_D\Bigl(\sigma\sqrt{1+|\nabla h|^2}
   +\tfrac12\Delta\rho\,g\,h^2-t\,h\Bigr)\dd x\dd y
   -\lambda\Bigl(\int_Dh\dd x\dd y-V_0\Bigr),
 \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
for a load $t$ that is held fixed as a function of $(x,y)$ during the
variation. This is the standard capillary variational structure
\cite{finn1986} with an added dead load; the treatment of acoustic loads that
depend on the shape is deferred to \cref{sec:formation}.

\begin{proposition}[Load demand]\label{prop:demand}
Let $h_\star\in C^2(\bar D)$ satisfy the pinning condition on $\partial D$ and
the volume constraint. Then $h_\star$ is a stationary point of
\cref{eq:lagrangian} if and only if
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\;t=\mathcal D[h_\star]-\lambda\quad\text{in }D\;}
 \label{eq:demand}
\end{equation}
for some constant $\lambda$. The function $t_\star=\mathcal D[h_\star]$, the
\emph{load demand} of the target, is explicit; the traction that holds $h_\star$
is unique up to the additive constant $\lambda$, which the volume constraint
absorbs.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For admissible variations $\delta h$ vanishing on $\partial D$, integration by
parts of the area term gives
$\delta\mathscr L=\int_D(\mathcal D[h_\star]-t-\lambda)\delta h\dd x\dd y$,
and variation of $\lambda$ restores the volume constraint. The bracket must
vanish for all such $\delta h$, which is \cref{eq:demand}. Conversely, any two
tractions holding the same $h_\star$ differ by $\lambda-\lambda'$, a constant.
\end{proof}

The demand is a property of the target and the liquid pair only. It does not
depend on the frame, provided the frame is compatible with the target's
boundary trace. Equation \cref{eq:demand} is the same as the required traction
of the canonical theory, $\Pi_{\rm req}=\sigma\kappa+\Delta\rho\Phi-\lambda_V$
with $\kappa=-\nabla\cdot(\nabla h/\sqrt{1+|\nabla h|^2})$ and $\Phi=gz$; it
also equals the Lagrange-multiplier field that enforces the prescribed geometry
pointwise. The equivalence of the multiplier and the required load is the
reason the inverse map in this direction is explicit rather than a solution of
a partial differential equation. For a surface with overhangs the same result
holds with $\sigma\kappa+\Delta\rho g z$ evaluated on a parametric surface.

Three practical consequences follow. First, unforced fluidic shaping is the
special case $t=\text{const}$: \cref{sec:reach} is the statement that
$t_\star$ must be constant there. Second, a load can only be bounded where the
target is $C^2$: a slope jump, for instance at the edge of an optical pupil
joined to an arbitrary outer annulus, demands a line force. The non-optical
annulus between pupil and frame is therefore a design variable that should be
chosen with matched slope and curvature. Third, $t_\star$ involves second
derivatives of the target. A height feature of amplitude $\delta h$ and lateral
wavenumber $k_\perp$ on a nearly flat surface demands a load of order
$\sigma k_\perp^2\delta h$, so fine detail is expensive regardless of how the
load is produced.

\subsection{Boundary control and interior control}\label{sec:decomp}
In the small-slope limit the operator is linear,
$\mathcal D[h]\simeq\mathcal L h$ with $\mathcal L=-\sigma\Delta+\Delta\rho g$,
and a target splits into two parts, each with its own actuator:
\begin{equation}
 h_\star=h_B+h_L,\qquad
 \mathcal Lh_B=\lambda_B,\ \ h_B|_{\partial D}=h_\star|_{\partial D};\qquad
 \mathcal Lh_L=t_\star-\lambda_B,\ \ h_L|_{\partial D}=0 .
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The part $h_B$ is fluidic shaping: frame trace plus volume. The part $h_L$ is
driven by the interior residual of the target and must be supplied as a load.
The two are complementary; neither replaces the other. A frame cannot create
an interior residual, and a bounded load cannot change the pinned trace. In
the cylindrical cell considered later the frame is a circular pinning edge of
constant height, so the frame contributes only the volume mode and the load
must supply all the rest. Combining an azimuthally shaped frame
\cite{elgarisi2021preprint} with an applied load is a natural option that this
paper does not develop.

\subsection{Two faces, three liquids}\label{sec:twoface}
A lens has two faces. Number the liquids from bottom to top $0,1,2$, with
the lens liquid $1$ between the front face $z=h_f$ and the back face
$z=h_b$, both pinned. Use upward normals on both faces and upward tractions
$t_f,t_b$. The density steps are $\Delta\rho_f=\rho_0-\rho_1$ and
$\Delta\rho_b=\rho_1-\rho_2$, and the tensions $\sigma_f,\sigma_b$. In a
sealed cell, the volume of liquid $0$ fixes $\int_Dh_f$ and the lens volume
fixes $\int_D(h_b-h_f)$; the volume of liquid $2$ then follows. With
multipliers $p_0$ and $p_1$ for these two constraints, the argument of
\cref{prop:demand} gives
\begin{equation}
 t_f=\mathcal D_f[h_f]-(p_0-p_1),\qquad
 t_b=\mathcal D_b[h_b]-p_1,
 \label{eq:two-face}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D_j$ uses $\sigma_j$ and $\Delta\rho_j$. The two constants
$\lambda_f=p_0-p_1$ and $\lambda_b=p_1$ are independent, so each face has its
own demand, unique up to its own constant. This is the structure of the
Frumkin--Bercovici energy with its lens-volume and enclosed-volume multipliers
\cite{frumkin2021}, with liquids $0$ and $2$ both equal to the immersion liquid
in their experiments.

If instead liquids $0$ and $2$ are one hydraulically connected bath of density
$\rho_e$, only the lens volume is constrained. Then
$\lambda_f=-p_1=-\lambda_b$: one shared constant, so the relative level of the
two loads is physical and sets the lens thickness. Subtracting a separate mean
from each face would discard it. Moreover $\Delta\rho_f=\rho_e-\rho_1=
-\Delta\rho_b$, so whenever the lens is not density matched one of its faces is
inversely stratified. This is the origin of the $J_0$/$I_0$ pair of
\cite{frumkin2021}.

\subsection{Existence, uniqueness and sign}\label{sec:uniqueness}
For the demand itself, existence is trivial and uniqueness holds modulo the
constants of \cref{sec:twoface}. The converse question, whether a prescribed
dead load produces a unique shape, depends on stratification.

\begin{proposition}[Uniqueness under a dead load]\label{prop:convex}
Under a load $t(x,y)$ held fixed in horizontal position, with pinned
boundaries, fixed volumes, $\sigma_j>0$ and $\Delta\rho_j\ge0$, the energy
\cref{eq:lagrangian}, summed over the faces, is strictly convex on the convex
set of admissible graphs. A stationary graph is therefore its unique global
minimizer.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The integrand $\sqrt{1+|\bm p|^2}$ is strictly convex in $\bm p$, the term
$\frac12\Delta\rho gh^2$ is convex for $\Delta\rho\ge0$, the load and volume
terms are linear, and the pinning excludes nonzero gradient-free variations.
\end{proof}

The canonical theory uses the same convexity argument; it holds for every
angular order of perturbation, not only axisymmetric ones. Inverse stratification,
$\Delta\rho_j<0$, removes it. A small-slope estimate shows how far capillarity
still protects the face. For a nearly flat pinned face whose mean displacement
is fixed by a sealed volume, the second variation
$\int_D(\sigma|\nabla\eta|^2+\Delta\rho g\eta^2)\dd x\dd y$ is positive for all
zero-mean $\eta$ vanishing on the circle of radius $R$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Bo}=\frac{|\Delta\rho|gR^2}{\sigma}<j_{1,1}^2\approx14.68 ,
 \label{eq:rt-threshold}
\end{equation}
where $j_{1,1}\approx3.8317$ is the first zero of $J_1$. The minimizing
perturbation is $J_1(j_{1,1}r/R)\cos\theta$, which has zero mean
automatically; the best axisymmetric zero-mean competitor has the larger value
$j_{2,1}^2\approx26.37$. This elementary bound, derived here, has two
consequences. It is consistent with the stability at $\mathrm{Bo}\sim1$ reported
in \cite{frumkin2021}. And the first mode to lose passive stiffness on an
inverted face is non-axisymmetric, which an axisymmetric analysis cannot see.

Sign constraints on the load are a separate matter. The free constant means
that a pointwise sign restriction of the actuator, such as a load that can only
push, is not by itself an obstruction: it costs a uniform offset, which has its
own price in field amplitude and absolute pressure. The real restriction is the
set of load \emph{patterns} the actuator can produce. For acoustic actuation
this set is characterized in \cref{sec:capability}, where the demand must lie in
a convex cone; positivity of a demand is then a condition to be tested, not an
automatic property.

\section{Series form near the axis and the conic matching rule}\label{sec:series}

For an axisymmetric face, $h(r)$, write $\phi=h'/\sqrt{1+h'^2}$; then
$\mathcal D[h]=-\sigma r^{-1}(r\phi)'+\Delta\rho gh$. Expand the sag as
$h=h_0+\tfrac12cr^2+a_4r^4+a_6r^6+\dots$, with vertex curvature $c$ in
$\mathrm m^{-1}$, $a_4$ in $\mathrm m^{-3}$ and $a_6$ in $\mathrm m^{-5}$.

\begin{proposition}[Series demand]\label{prop:series}
\begin{equation}
 t_\star(r)=(\Delta\rho gh_0-2\sigma c)+Q_2r^2+Q_4r^4+O(r^6),
 \qquad
 \begin{aligned}
 Q_2&=-\sigma\,(16a_4-2c^3)+\tfrac12\Delta\rho\,g\,c,\\
 Q_4&=-\sigma\,\bigl(36a_6-36c^2a_4+\tfrac94c^5\bigr)+\Delta\rho\,g\,a_4 .
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:series}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $h'=cr+4a_4r^3+6a_6r^5+\dots$ and
$\phi=h'-\tfrac12h'^3+\tfrac38h'^5+\dots$, one finds
$\phi=cr+(4a_4-\tfrac12c^3)r^3+(6a_6-6c^2a_4+\tfrac38c^5)r^5+\dots$.
Then $r^{-1}(r\phi)'=2c+(16a_4-2c^3)r^2+(36a_6-36c^2a_4+\tfrac94c^5)r^4+\dots$;
adding the gravity term gives \cref{eq:series}.
\end{proof}
The constant is absorbed by the volume multiplier, so $Q_2$ and $Q_4$, in
$\mathrm{Pa\,m^{-2}}$ and $\mathrm{Pa\,m^{-4}}$, are the leading demand. Both
vanish for a sphere ($a_4=c^3/8$, $a_6=c^5/16$) when $\Delta\rho=0$, as they
must. We checked the coefficients, and \cref{prop:conic} below, in exact
rational arithmetic, independently of the project's implementation.

For a conic face, $h=cr^2/\bigl(1+\sqrt{1-(1+k)c^2r^2}\bigr)$ with conic
constant $k$, the coefficients are $a_4=(1+k)c^3/8$ and
$a_6=(1+k)^2c^5/16$. Then $Q_2=-2\sigma kc^3+\tfrac12\Delta\rho gc$, and the
leading demand vanishes when
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\frac{\Delta\rho\,g}{\sigma}=4\,k\,c^2 .}
 \label{eq:matching}
\end{equation}
This \emph{matching rule} chooses the liquid pair so that gravity supplies the
fourth-order departure of the face from a sphere. Its sign is that of $k$: an
oblate-ellipsoidal face ($k>0$) needs the denser liquid below, while prolate
ellipsoidal, paraboloidal and hyperboloidal faces ($k<0$) need inverse
stratification, and then
\cref{eq:rt-threshold} applies with $\mathrm{Bo}=4|k|c^2R^2$. For an even
asphere with additional coefficient $A_4$ on top of the conic,
$a_4=(1+k)c^3/8+A_4$, the rule becomes
$\Delta\rho g/\sigma=4kc^2+32A_4/c$.

\begin{proposition}[Conic remainder]\label{prop:conic}
Under \cref{eq:matching},
\begin{equation}
 Q_4=\tfrac14\,\sigma\,k\,(2-7k)\,c^5 .
 \label{eq:conic-remainder}
\end{equation}
Consequently a conic graph with $c\neq0$ is an unforced capillary--gravity
equilibrium near its axis only if $k=0$ and $\Delta\rho=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Substituting $a_4,a_6$ and $\Delta\rho g=4\sigma kc^2$ into \cref{eq:series}
gives $36a_6-36c^2a_4+\tfrac94c^5=\tfrac94k^2c^5$ and
$\Delta\rho ga_4=\tfrac12\sigma k(1+k)c^5$, whose difference is
\cref{eq:conic-remainder}. An unforced equilibrium requires $Q_2=0$, hence
\cref{eq:matching}, and $Q_4=0$, hence $k\in\{0,\tfrac27\}$. For $k=0$ the
rule gives $\Delta\rho=0$. For $k=\tfrac27$ the next coefficient is
$Q_6=\tfrac{41}{686}\sigma c^7\neq0$.
\end{proof}

So the matching rule can remove the leading demand but not all of it; for a
conic the remainder starts at $r^4$ with the explicit coefficient
\cref{eq:conic-remainder}. The pupil of an optical face is a small part of
$D$ in many designs, and there the series is a quick guide to which liquid pair
leaves the smallest load. For two faces in a three-liquid cell, the two rules
are independent because $\Delta\rho_f$ and $\Delta\rho_b$ involve different
outer liquids. In a single connected bath, $\Delta\rho_f=-\Delta\rho_b$, and if
$\sigma_f=\sigma_b$ the two rules can hold together only when $k_f$ and $k_b$
have opposite signs and $|k_f|c_f^2=|k_b|c_b^2$.

As an illustration, in the project's study S09 the exact demand
\cref{eq:demand} applied as a dead load to a finite-element meniscus
reproduced two published stigmatic singlets \cite{silva2020} to
$3\times10^{-11}$\,m. The series matched the exact demand to $6\times10^{-4}$
within $r\le0.15R$. And \cref{eq:matching} predicted the brine and oil pair that
an earlier brute-force scan had selected
($-8.3\times10^4$ versus $-7.9\times10^4\,\mathrm m^{-2}$ for $\Delta\rho g/\sigma$
on the front face; $1.0\times10^6$ versus $7.5\times10^5\,\mathrm m^{-2}$ on the
back).

\Cref{fig:cell} shows the apparatus of the remaining sections: a rigid
cylinder of radius $R$, three liquids, two pinned faces, and prescribed
normal velocity on the end windows and on wall bands. We work in the following
regime, each item of which is an approximation stated in the canonical theory
and revisited in \cref{sec:validity}. The acoustic field is harmonic at angular
frequency $\omega$ and settles fast compared with the interface motion, so that
cycle averages are taken at fixed geometry. Bulk liquids are inviscid and
lossless; dissipation enters only through a declared admittance on parts of the
boundary. The interface is treated in the high-frequency transmission
reduction: acoustic pressure and normal velocity are continuous across it,
which neglects first-order capillary and gravity restoring terms at the carrier
frequency \cite{chesneau2022}. Sources are ideal: their normal velocity is
prescribed regardless of acoustic loading. Everything is axisymmetric.

With the peak-phasor convention $p_1=\Rea\{Pe^{-\mathrm i\omega t}\}$ and
$\bm v_1=\Rea\{\bm Ve^{-\mathrm i\omega t}\}$, each liquid $\ell$ with density
$\rho_\ell$ and sound speed $c_\ell$ obeys
$-\mathrm i\omega\rho_\ell\bm V=-\nabla P$ and
$\nabla\cdot(\rho_\ell^{-1}\nabla P)+\omega^2(\rho_\ell c_\ell^2)^{-1}P=0$.
On the source boundary the outward normal velocity is
$\bm V\cdot\bm n_a=YP+v_s$, with $v_s$ the source velocity and $Y$ the declared
admittance ($Y=0$ on rigid or ideal-source parts). Away from resonances of the
cell this linear problem is well posed; a closed lossless cavity at an
eigenfrequency is excluded.

\subsection{Normal radiation traction}
For this reduction the cycle-averaged acoustic momentum flux in liquid $\ell$
is \cite{chesneau2022}
\begin{equation}
 \bm R_\ell=\Bigl(\frac{|P|^2}{4\rho_\ell c_\ell^2}
            -\frac{\rho_\ell|\bm V|^2}{4}\Bigr)\Id
   +\frac{\rho_\ell}{2}\Rea(\bm V\otimes\bm V^*),
 \label{eq:momentum-flux}
\end{equation}
which is minus the radiation-stress convention of \cite{chesneau2022}. The
cycle-averaged traction on the interface along the upward normal $\nn$, from
the lower liquid ($-$) to the upper ($+$), is
$\Pi=\nn\cdot(\bm R_--\bm R_+)\nn$; it enters the interface balance as the
load $t$ of \cref{sec:demand}. Writing $V_n=\bm V\cdot\nn$, the surface
gradient $\Gs$, and the compressibility $\beta_\ell=1/(\rho_\ell c_\ell^2)$, the
continuity of $P$, $V_n$ and $\Gs P$ across the interface gives (derived in
\cref{app:traction})
\begin{equation}
 \Pi=\tfrac14(\beta_--\beta_+)|P|^2
    +\tfrac14(\rho_--\rho_+)|V_n|^2
    +\frac{1}{4\omega^2}\Bigl(\frac1{\rho_+}-\frac1{\rho_-}\Bigr)|\Gs P|^2 .
 \label{eq:traction}
\end{equation}
Each term has a sign fixed by a material contrast, and the three contrasts need
not agree, so the local stress can push or pull depending on the field
structure. Two independent checks follow. For a plane wave crossing an
impedance-matched interface at normal incidence ($\rho_-c_-=\rho_+c_+$, so
$V_n=P/(\rho c)$ on both sides and $\Gs P=0$), \cref{eq:traction} becomes
$\Pi=\tfrac12(\beta_--\beta_+)|P|^2$, the difference of the mean acoustic energy
densities on the two sides. This is the Langevin radiation pressure on a
transparent interface, which Issenmann \emph{et al.} found to describe small
deformations of a transparent chloroform--water interface
\cite{issenmann2008}. In the one-sided pressure-release limit with negligible
tangential velocity, \cref{eq:momentum-flux} instead gives
$\Pi\simeq\rho_-|V_n|^2/4$, the canonical theory's limit. Deformation of liquid
interfaces by acoustic radiation pressure is established experimentally and
numerically \cite{issenmann2008,bertin2012,chesneau2022,sisombat2023}; what
follows uses it as a distributed actuator.

For scale, with $p_{\max}=1$\,MPa in water ($\rho c^2\approx2.2$\,GPa),
$p_{\max}^2/(4\rho c^2)\approx1\times10^2$\,Pa, multiplied by contrast factors
of order $|\beta_--\beta_+|/\beta$. Demands of the illustrative singlets in S09
are of order $0.1$--$1$\,Pa. The required load is therefore a small, structured
part of the available stress, which is why parameter sensitivity and the
precision of the wave model matter.

\subsection{The force is a Hermitian form in the source amplitudes}
Let the source velocity be a finite combination of fixed real boundary
functions, $v_s=\sum_{k=1}^mc_k\psi_k$ with $\bm c\in\C^m$; the canonical
choice is Neumann--Bessel modes on each window and cosines along the wall, each
normalized so that $|c_k|$ is a velocity amplitude in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.
Finite arrays are restrictions of this continuous source space. By linearity,
$P=\sum_kc_kP_k$, and likewise $V_n$ and $\Gs P$. Inserting into
\cref{eq:traction} gives $\Pi(\bm x)=\bm c^{\mathsf H}\bm Q(\bm x)\bm c$ with a
Hermitian matrix field $\bm Q$.

Let $q\in\R^n$ be coordinates of both faces in a space that already enforces
the pinned rims and the conserved volumes, with dimensionless basis functions
$\varphi_i$ for normal displacement. The energy $E(q)$ has gradient
$\nabla E$ in newtons and Hessian $K=\nabla^2E$. The generalized acoustic force
on coordinate $i$ is
\begin{equation}
 F_i(q;\bm c)=\int_\Gamma\varphi_i\,\Pi\dd A=\bm c^{\mathsf H}G_i(q)\,\bm c,
 \qquad G_i=\int_\Gamma\varphi_i\bm Q\dd A=G_i^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:hermitian-force}
\end{equation}
In these coordinates the load demand of \cref{prop:demand} is the vector
$\bm d=\nabla E(q_\star)$, already free of the volume constants, and an
acoustic drive holds the target exactly when $F(q_\star;\bm c)=\bm d$.
The global phase $\bm c\mapsto e^{\mathrm i\vartheta}\bm c$ leaves every force
unchanged, and $G_i$ is indefinite in general, consistent with
\cref{eq:traction}. The source power is also a Hermitian form,
$\mathcal P=\bm c^{\mathsf H}W\bm c$: in this lossless-bulk model it equals
the power absorbed by the declared boundary admittance. Below we assume
$W\succ0$, meaning that every nonzero drive dissipates power. Peak pressure at
a sample point $s$ is $|p_s|=|\bm p_s^{\mathsf H}\bm c|$ for a fixed row
$\bm p_s$.

Finding $\bm c$ with $\bm c^{\mathsf H}G_i\bm c=d_i$ for all $i$ is a system
of indefinite quadratic equations: nonconvex, possibly without solutions, and
with spurious local minima for least-squares solvers. In the project's
study S08 a single-tone inverse started from random seeds stalled at
$0.67$\,\textmu m for one published singlet and was read as a reachability
limit; the convex formulation below later showed that this was a local minimum
(S09). The next section removes the nonconvexity at the cost of using more
than one tone.

\section{Convex capability of a fixed source}\label{sec:capability}

\subsection{Multi-tone drives give a convex cone}\label{sec:multitone}
Drive the same source modes with several tones,
$v_s(\bm x,t)=\Rea\sum_\mu\sum_kc_{\mu k}\psi_k(\bm x)e^{-\mathrm i\omega_\mu t}$.
Products of tones $\mu\neq\nu$ oscillate at $\omega_\mu-\omega_\nu$. If
every such difference is far above the bandwidth of the interface response
(in creeping flow, its slowest relaxation rates; of order
$10\,\mathrm s^{-1}$ in S09), and far enough from mechanical resonances of the
interface, the interface responds only to the sum of single-tone averages,
$F_i=\sum_\mu\bm c_\mu^{\mathsf H}G_i(\omega_\mu)\bm c_\mu$. Group the tones
into bands $t$ in each of which the kernels are approximately constant,
$G_i(\omega_\mu)\simeq G_{t,i}$; this requires the tone spread in a band to be
small compared with the frequency scale on which the cell response varies, for
example the width of a nearby resonance. Then
\begin{equation}
 F_i(X)=\sum_t\tr(G_{t,i}X_t),\qquad
 X_t=\sum_{\mu\in t}\bm c_\mu\bm c_\mu^{\mathsf H}\succeq0,\qquad
 \mathcal P(X)=\sum_t\tr(W_tX_t).
 \label{eq:multitone}
\end{equation}
Conversely, any $X_t\succeq0$ of rank $r_t$ is realized by $r_t$ tones in band
$t$ through its eigendecomposition. The reachable forces
$\mathcal K=\{F(X):X_t\succeq0\}$ therefore form a convex cone, the linear
image of a product of positive semidefinite cones. Single-tone drives are the
rank-one points. Fast time-multiplexing between single-tone commands, slower
than acoustic ring-down and faster than the interface, gives convex
combinations of single-tone forces as well; it is an alternative realization of
the same cone with its own transient errors.

\subsection{Exact reachability and its certificate}
For a vector $\bm a\in\R^n$ write $G_{t,\bm a}=\sum_ia_iG_{t,i}$.
\begin{proposition}[Theorem of alternatives]\label{prop:alternatives}
(i) Without a power limit, $\bm d$ lies in the closure of $\mathcal K$ if and
only if $\bm a^{\mathsf T}\bm d\ge0$ for every $\bm a$ with
$G_{t,\bm a}\succeq0$ for all $t$.
(ii) With $W_t\succ0$ and power cap $P_{\max}$, the set
$\mathcal K_{P}=\{F(X):X_t\succeq0,\ \mathcal P(X)\le P_{\max}\}$ is compact
and convex, and $\bm d\in\mathcal K_P$ if and only if, for every $\bm a$,
\begin{equation}
 \bm a^{\mathsf T}\bm d\le P_{\max}\max_t\,\lambda_{\max}^+\bigl(
   W_t^{-1/2}G_{t,\bm a}W_t^{-1/2}\bigr),
 \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\max}^+=\max(0,\lambda_{\max})$. The least multi-tone power
that holds $\bm d$ is the supremum over $\bm a$ of the ratio of the two sides
with $P_{\max}=1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) $\bm a^{\mathsf T}F(X)=\sum_t\tr(G_{t,\bm a}X_t)$, which is nonnegative
for all $X_t\succeq0$ exactly when every $G_{t,\bm a}\succeq0$. This set of
$\bm a$ is the dual cone of $\mathcal K$, and the bipolar theorem gives the
closure of $\mathcal K$. (ii) $W_t\succ0$ bounds $\tr X_t$ on the feasible
set, which is closed, so $\mathcal K_P$ is compact. With
$Y_t=W_t^{1/2}X_tW_t^{1/2}$, the maximum of the linear function
$\sum_t\tr(W_t^{-1/2}G_{t,\bm a}W_t^{-1/2}Y_t)$ over $Y_t\succeq0$,
$\sum_t\tr Y_t\le P_{\max}$, is the right-hand side of \cref{eq:support}. This
is the support function of $\mathcal K_P$, and a point belongs to a compact
convex set if and only if it satisfies all its supporting half-spaces.
\end{proof}
A vector $\bm a$ violating \cref{eq:support} is a certificate that no drive in
those bands, single-tone or multi-tone, holds the target within the power
cap. For single tones the same inequality is only necessary, as in the
canonical source-effort bounds; for multi-tone drives it is also sufficient.
The closure in (i) is needed because the image of a semidefinite cone need not
be closed. The certificate also makes precise the remark of
\cref{sec:uniqueness}: sign or positivity of a demand is the conic condition
(i), and it is not automatic.

\subsection{The best-tolerance program}
An exact match is neither expected nor needed. Let $H$ sample the pupils of both
faces, and use the frozen first-order response at the target,
$\delta\bm h=O(F-\bm d)+V\bm v$ with $O=HK^{-1}$. Here $\bm v$ are trims of
the fill volumes (the pumps are part of the machine) and $V$ their height
columns. The best pupil tolerance within the caps is
\begin{equation}
 \tau_\star=\min_{X_t\succeq0,\ \bm v,\ \tau}\ \tau
 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
 \bigl|\bigl(O(F(X)-\bm d)+V\bm v\bigr)_k\bigr|\le\tau\ \forall k,\quad
 \mathcal P(X)\le P_{\max},
 \label{eq:sdp}
\end{equation}
The pressure and fold constraints of \cref{sec:convex-constraints} can be
added. Problem \cref{eq:sdp} is a semidefinite program, which interior-point
methods solve to global optimality \cite{vandenberghe1996}.
Its optimum is a lower bound on the pupil error of every drive in the given
bands, in the frozen model.

\begin{proposition}[Dual certificate]\label{prop:dual}
For any $\bm y$ with $\norm{\bm y}_1\le1$ and $V^{\mathsf T}\bm y=0$, and any
$\nu\ge0$ with $G_{t,\bm a}\preceq\nu W_t$ for all $t$, where
$\bm a=O^{\mathsf T}\bm y$,
\begin{equation}
 \tau_\star\ \ge\ \bm a^{\mathsf T}\bm d-\nu P_{\max}.
 \label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
Since $X_t=\epsilon\Id$ is strictly feasible for small $\epsilon>0$, Slater's
condition holds, there is no duality gap, and the best such bound equals
$\tau_\star$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For feasible $(X,\bm v,\tau)$, the height constraints and $V^{\mathsf T}\bm y=0$
give $\bm y^{\mathsf T}O(\bm d-F(X))\le\norm{\bm y}_1\tau\le\tau$, so
$\tau\ge\bm a^{\mathsf T}\bm d-\sum_t\tr(G_{t,\bm a}X_t)
\ge\bm a^{\mathsf T}\bm d-\nu\,\mathcal P(X)\ge\bm a^{\mathsf T}\bm d-\nu P_{\max}$.
Strong duality under Slater's condition is standard \cite{vandenberghe1996}.
\end{proof}
When $\tau_\star$ exceeds the goal, the dual pair is a checkable proof that the
source layout cannot hold the lens in this model; the remedy is more source
freedom (bands, modes, placement), not more optimizer iterations. The
certificate is a statement about the frozen linearization at $q_\star$. It
neglects the acoustic shape stiffness $A=\partial_qF$, which S09 estimated at
$\norm A/\norm K\sim10^{-4}$ for its cell, and the start-up path; both are
treated in \cref{sec:formation}.

\subsection{How many tones}
\begin{proposition}[Rank of an optimal drive]\label{prop:rank}
Assume $W_t\succ0$ and no fold constraint. There is an optimal solution of
\cref{eq:sdp} whose ranks satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \sum_tr_t^2\le n+1+n_p ,
 \label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of generalized forces and $n_p$ the number of
pressure caps active at that solution.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix an optimal $X^\star$ and let $\mathcal S$ be the set of $X\succeq0$ with
$F(X)=F(X^\star)$, $\mathcal P(X)=\mathcal P(X^\star)$, and all pressure caps.
Every element of $\mathcal S$ is optimal with the same $\bm v,\tau$, and
$\mathcal S$ is compact and convex, so it has an extreme point $X^e$. The face
of the product cone at $X^e$ consists of $U_tZ_tU_t^{\mathsf H}$ with
$Z_t$ Hermitian of size $r_t$, a real vector space of dimension
$\sum_tr_t^2$. If this exceeded the number of real equality and active
inequality constraints, a nonzero direction in the face would satisfy all of
them, and $X^e\pm\epsilon D\in\mathcal S$ for small $\epsilon$, contradicting
extremality.
\end{proof}
This is the Hermitian form of the dimension count of Barvinok and Pataki, who
state it for real symmetric matrices as $r(r+1)/2\le m$
\cite{barvinok1995,pataki1998}; see \cite{luo2010} for its role in
semidefinite relaxation. The bound is weak when the face space is rich, since
$n$ can be large. It does not promise a single tone. A rank-one optimum, when it
occurs, is an observation; otherwise the leading eigenvector of $X_t^\star$
initializes a local, nonconvex refinement to one tone per band. In S09 the
convex bound for one published singlet was below $10^{-11}$\,m at 0.46\,W, and
its leading eigenvector refined to a single tone of 0.17\,W. For 68 singlets
of a stigmatic family in one fixed cell the bound was below 0.5\,nm within
1\,W, and a refined command with one tone per band, using one or two bands,
met the 10\,nm first-order goal for each of them.

\subsection{Pressure caps and fold margin are convex}\label{sec:convex-constraints}
The squared pressure amplitude of band $t$ at sample $s$ is
$\bm p_{t,s}^{\mathsf H}X_t\bm p_{t,s}$, linear in $X_t$. A cap
$\bm p_{t,s}^{\mathsf H}X_t\bm p_{t,s}\le p_{\max}^2$ is exact for one tone per
band and a root-mean-square bound for several tones. The acoustic shape
stiffness is linear in the drive as well,
$A_{ij}(X)=\sum_t\tr(\partial_{q_j}G_{t,i}X_t)$, and need not be symmetric.

\begin{proposition}[Fold margin and creeping-flow stability]\label{prop:lmi}
Let $K$ be symmetric positive definite, $A$ real, and $0<\mu\le1$. If
\begin{equation}
 \sym(K-A)\succeq\mu K,\qquad \sym(B)=\tfrac12(B+B^{\mathsf T}),
 \label{eq:lmi}
\end{equation}
then (i) every eigenvalue of $K^{-1}(K-A)$ has real part at least $\mu$, so
$K-A$ is nonsingular (no fold); (ii) in the $K$-energy norm the coupled
response $(K-A)^{-1}\delta F$ is at most $1/\mu$ times the frozen response
$K^{-1}\delta F$; (iii) for every symmetric positive definite mobility $M$, the
creeping-flow linearization $\dot{\bm\chi}=-M(K-A)\bm\chi$ is asymptotically
stable with decay rate at least $\mu\,\lambda_{\min}(M^{1/2}KM^{1/2})$, that is,
$\mu$ times the slowest passive relaxation rate.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) If $(K-A)\bm x=\lambda K\bm x$ with $\bm x\ne0$, then
$\bm x^{\mathsf H}(K-A)\bm x=\lambda\,\bm x^{\mathsf H}K\bm x$; the real part
of the left side is $\bm x^{\mathsf H}\sym(K-A)\bm x\ge\mu\,\bm x^{\mathsf H}K\bm x$.
(ii) If $(K-A)\bm y=K\bm z$, then
$\mu\norm{\bm y}_K^2\le\bm y^{\mathsf T}(K-A)\bm y=\bm y^{\mathsf T}K\bm z
\le\norm{\bm y}_K\norm{\bm z}_K$. (iii) If $M(K-A)\bm x=\lambda\bm x$, then
$\bm x^{\mathsf H}(K-A)\bm x=\lambda\,\bm x^{\mathsf H}M^{-1}\bm x$, and taking
real parts gives
$\Rea\lambda\ge\mu\,\bm x^{\mathsf H}K\bm x/\bm x^{\mathsf H}M^{-1}\bm x
\ge\mu\lambda_{\min}(M^{1/2}KM^{1/2})$.
\end{proof}
Because $A(X)$ is linear in $X$, \cref{eq:lmi} is a linear matrix inequality
in the drive \cite{boyd1994} and can be added to \cref{eq:sdp}. It is
sufficient, not necessary: it guarantees a margin at the target, independent
of the unknown viscous mobility, but says nothing about the path to the target.
The weaker condition that the eigenvalues of $K^{-1}(K-A)$ be positive does not
by itself give (iii) for a non-symmetric $A$. In S09 an unconstrained single
tone for one published singlet lay within 7\,\% of a fold (margin 0.068,
frozen-to-coupled amplification 18.5); with \cref{eq:lmi} at $\mu=0.7$ the
refined tone had margin 0.94 and amplification 1.09 at similar power. The
constraint can also cost accuracy: for another lens of that survey it made the
pupil error larger, not smaller.

\section{From the meniscus to the held state}\label{sec:formation}

\subsection{A held equilibrium is not automatically reached}
Under a fixed drive, the equilibria solve
\begin{equation}
 F(q;\bm c)=\nabla E(q).
 \label{eq:held}
\end{equation}
The inverse fixes the force only at $q_\star$; away from it, $F(q;\bm c)$ is
whatever the wave field produces. A standing-wave stress varies on the length
$\pi/k_{\rm ac}$, half an acoustic wavelength, so \cref{eq:held} typically has
several solutions, separated by displacements of order a quarter wavelength. The
physical start is the acoustics-off equilibrium $q_0$, $\nabla E(q_0)=0$, at
the declared fill volumes: exactly the fluidic-shaping meniscus of
\cref{sec:fluidic}. Starting the drive as $s(t)\bm c$ with a ramp $s$ from 0
to 1 scales every force by $s^2$, and the start-up branch $q_{\mathcal B}(s)$
solves
\begin{equation}
 s^2F(q_{\mathcal B};\bm c)=\nabla E(q_{\mathcal B}),\qquad
 q_{\mathcal B}(0)=q_0,\qquad
 \bigl(K-s^2A\bigr)\frac{\dd q_{\mathcal B}}{\dd s}=2sF .
 \label{eq:branch}
\end{equation}
If $K-s^2A$ becomes singular at some $s_f<1$, the branch folds and the state
jumps to another attractor. The branch is a property of the physical start-up,
not continuation in the target, and it is checked independently by integrating
the forward dynamics from $q_0$. In creeping flow, a slow ramp keeps the state
within $O(\epsilon/\lambda)$ of a uniformly stable branch, with ramp rate
$\epsilon$ and decay margin $\lambda$; this is the standard slow-manifold
estimate \cite{fenichel1979} used in the canonical theory. In S09, one lens of
the 68-lens survey shows the failure plainly: the two-tone drive holds the
target in the frozen model (0.33\,nm, convex bound 0.02\,nm), yet the start-up
branch from the meniscus ends on another equilibrium, 25 and
159\,\textmu m from the target faces. The limit was the path, not the
capability.

Choosing the fill volumes so that the meniscus is as close as possible to the
target reduces the acoustic task to correcting the residual asphericity. It
also scales down the first-order parameter shifts, which are proportional to
the acoustic load, and shortens the path.

\subsection{A sufficient condition for reaching the target}
The following condition, derived here, turns the pointwise fold margin of
\cref{prop:lmi} into a statement about the path.
\begin{proposition}[Monotone start-up]\label{prop:reach}
Let $\mathcal C\subset\R^n$ be convex and contain $q_0$ and $q_\star$, and let
$F(\cdot;\bm c)$ and $\nabla E$ be $C^1$ on $\mathcal C$. Suppose that for all
$q\in\mathcal C$, $K(q)\succeq\kappa\Id$ with $\kappa>0$ and
\begin{equation}
 \sym\bigl(K(q)-A(q;\bm c)\bigr)\succeq\mu K(q),\qquad 0<\mu\le1 .
 \label{eq:region-lmi}
\end{equation}
If the closed ball of radius $\norm{F(q_0;\bm c)}/(\mu\kappa)$ about $q_0$
lies in the interior of $\mathcal C$, then the start-up branch exists for all
$s\in[0,1]$, has no fold, stays in that ball, and ends at the unique solution
of \cref{eq:held} in $\mathcal C$, which is $q_\star$ when $\bm c$ holds the
target.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal G_s(q)=\nabla E(q)-s^2F(q;\bm c)$. Its Jacobian satisfies
$\sym(K-s^2A)=(1-s^2)K+s^2\sym(K-A)\succeq(1-s^2+s^2\mu)K\succeq\mu\kappa\Id$,
so $\mathcal G_s$ is strongly monotone on $\mathcal C$ with constant
$m=\mu\kappa$. It therefore has at most one zero in $\mathcal C$, and its
Jacobian is nonsingular there. For a zero $q$ in the convex set
$\mathcal C$, monotonicity against $q_0$ gives
$m\norm{q-q_0}^2\le\inner{\mathcal G_s(q)-\mathcal G_s(q_0)}{q-q_0}
=s^2\inner{F(q_0;\bm c)}{q-q_0}$, hence
$\norm{q-q_0}\le s^2\norm{F(q_0;\bm c)}/m$. The set of $s\in[0,1]$ for which
the branch exists in $\mathcal C$ contains $0$, is open by the implicit function
theorem, and is closed because the a priori bound keeps limits inside the ball,
where the Jacobian is nonsingular. It is therefore all of $[0,1]$.
\end{proof}
Verifying \cref{eq:region-lmi} on a whole region is harder than at one point,
and the proposition is only sufficient. It explains the failure modes, though:
a fold or a jump to another well requires the acoustic stiffness to overcome a
fraction of the capillary stiffness somewhere between meniscus and target.
Because $A(q;X)$ is linear in $X$ for each $q$, imposing \cref{eq:region-lmi}
at sampled states along a planned path, for example the segment from $q_0$ to
$q_\star$, adds convex constraints to \cref{eq:sdp}. Sampling is not a proof,
and this path-margin program has not yet been tested.

\subsection{Stability in creeping flow}
In the creeping-flow reduction, $\dot q=M(q)\bigl(F(q;\bm c)-\nabla E(q)\bigr)$
with a symmetric positive definite mobility $M$, the viscous mobility of the
interfaces under the volume constraints. Its symmetry and positivity follow from
the reciprocal theorem and positive viscous dissipation, as for the resistance
matrices of rigid particles \cite{happel1983}. The linearization at a held state
is $\dot{\bm\chi}=-M(K-A)\bm\chi$. \Cref{prop:lmi}(iii) gives a
mobility-independent certificate of axisymmetric linear stability. Without it,
stability requires the eigenvalues of $M(K-A)$ to have positive real parts for
the actual $M$; a static fold test alone does not suffice when $A$ is not
symmetric, because a non-conservative acoustic stiffness can destabilize
through the mobility.

The scope of this statement is narrow. It concerns axisymmetric perturbations
in the reduced coordinates, with instantaneous acoustics and no fluid inertia.
The cylinder admits perturbations of every azimuthal order $m$; when the base
state is axisymmetric they decouple by order, and each sector needs its own
operator. The passive bound \cref{eq:rt-threshold} shows that on an inversely
stratified face the first mode to soften is an $m=1$ mode. Acoustic memory
replaces $A$ by a frequency-dependent response, and beat forcing of multi-tone
drives calls for a periodic (Floquet) analysis when the separation conditions
of \cref{sec:multitone} are only marginally met.

\section{Regime of validity and open questions}\label{sec:validity}

\paragraph{What is exact.} \Cref{prop:demand,prop:series,prop:conic} are
exact for constant $\sigma$, uniform gravity, quiescent liquids and pinned
graphs. \Cref{prop:alternatives,prop:dual,prop:rank,prop:lmi,prop:reach} are
exact statements about the stated finite-dimensional models.

\paragraph{What is approximated.} The acoustic model is linear, inviscid in
the bulk, cycle-averaged, harmonic with fast settling, with ideal velocity
sources and the high-frequency transmission reduction at the interfaces. The
multi-tone cone needs tone separation beyond the interface bandwidth and
frequency-flat kernels within a band. The capability program is a first-order,
frozen-compliance statement at the target. Formation and stability use an
axisymmetric creeping-flow reduction. The validity of each item is a matter of
scales (Mach number, boundary-layer thickness, ring-down time, Reynolds number,
Ohnesorge and Bond numbers) that must be checked for a given cell.

\paragraph{What is open.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \emph{Acoustic streaming.} Fields of the amplitude needed here drive
 steady streaming in the bulk and in viscous boundary layers
 \cite{lighthill1978,bach2018,bruus2012perturbation}. Its viscous stresses on
 the interfaces may be comparable to demands of $0.1$--$1$\,Pa, and a steady
 base flow changes both the equilibrium and its stability. A quiescent
 spectrum is not the stability of a streaming base flow.
 \item \emph{Heating.} Absorption heats the liquids, changing sound speeds,
 densities and interfacial tension, and gradients of $\sigma$ produce
 Marangoni stresses. S09 found coupled shifts of tens to hundreds of
 nanometres for a $0.1$\,\% change of one sound speed: a physical machine would
 need measurement and closed-loop trim.
 \item \emph{Three-dimensional disturbances.} Non-axisymmetric modes, with the
 $m=1$ mode singled out by \cref{eq:rt-threshold}, have not been analysed for
 driven states.
 \item \emph{Transducers.} Real sources are not ideal: the delivered velocity
 depends on acoustic loading, electrical drive and transducer dynamics, and
 holding a command fixed does not hold $v_s$ fixed. The modal source space
 must be realized by hardware with calibrated response.
 \item \emph{Curing.} Fluidic shaping cures the liquid
 \cite{frumkin2021,elgarisi2021}. With acoustic shaping, the field would have
 to be held during curing while density, sound speed and stiffness of the lens
 liquid change, which alters the load; shrinkage and residual stress then act on
 the solid. Driven liquid optics, transient formation and curing are distinct
 problems; this paper treats only the driven liquid equilibrium.
 \item \emph{Contact lines and inertia.} Pinning is assumed perfect; hysteresis
 and depinning, as well as inertial capillary oscillations during start-up, are
 outside the reduction.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Optics.} A held surface that matches a stigmatic target is
stigmatic on axis for the design conjugates only. Axial stigmatism does not
imply aplanatism or achromatism, and those require their own analysis.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
Fluidic shaping reaches the surfaces on which the capillary--gravity operator
is constant: spheres, Bessel aspheres and Fourier--Bessel freeforms selected by
frame, volume and Bond number. Adding a normal traction removes that
restriction, and the traction needed is explicit: the load demand, the residual
of the target in the capillary--gravity operator, unique up to one constant per
conserved volume. Near the axis the demand is a short series; a matching rule
can remove its leading term with the right liquid pair, and an explicit
remainder shows what is left. Acoustic radiation stress is a candidate actuator.
In a cylinder with ideal sources its generalized force is a Hermitian form, and
multi-tone drives make the capability question convex, with certificates of
impossibility, a bound on the number of tones, and convex constraints for
pressure and fold margin, the last of which also certifies creeping-flow
stability for any symmetric positive mobility. Holding a target and reaching it
from the meniscus are different conditions; a regional monotonicity condition
links them. All of this is theory in a declared reduction. Streaming, heating,
three-dimensional modes, real transducers and curing decide whether an
apparatus could work, and none has been built or measured.

\section{Normal radiation traction}\label{app:traction}
Write $\bm V=V_n\nn+\bm V_t$ with $\bm V_t\perp\nn$. From
\cref{eq:momentum-flux},
\[
 \nn\cdot\bm R_\ell\nn=\frac{|P|^2}{4\rho_\ell c_\ell^2}
   -\frac{\rho_\ell}{4}\bigl(|V_n|^2+|\bm V_t|^2\bigr)
   +\frac{\rho_\ell}{2}|V_n|^2
 =\frac{\beta_\ell}{4}|P|^2+\frac{\rho_\ell}{4}\bigl(|V_n|^2-|\bm V_t|^2\bigr).
\]
The momentum equation gives $\bm V_t=\Gs P/(\mathrm i\omega\rho_\ell)$, so
$\rho_\ell|\bm V_t|^2=|\Gs P|^2/(\omega^2\rho_\ell)$. In the transmission
reduction $P$, hence $\Gs P$, and $V_n$ are continuous across the interface.
Subtracting the $+$ side from the $-$ side gives \cref{eq:traction}. Each term
is $\bm c^{\mathsf H}(\cdot)\bm c$ for a Hermitian matrix because $P$, $V_n$
and $\Gs P$ depend linearly on $\bm c$; for example
$|P|^2=\bm c^{\mathsf H}\bm P^*\bm P^{\mathsf T}\bm c$ with
$\bm P=(P_1,\dots,P_m)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\bm P^*$ its complex conjugate.
